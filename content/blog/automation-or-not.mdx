Automation is worth building when a task is frequent, follows clear rules and costs real time. It is a poor investment when the process itself is still unclear.

## Write the manual process down first

If two people in your team describe the same process differently, fix the process before you automate it. Automation locks in the behaviour you encode. If that behaviour is inconsistent or wrong, the automation makes it consistently wrong — and faster.

A written process doesn't need to be a document. A short checklist or a linear sequence of steps is enough to test whether everyone agrees on what happens.

## Good candidates for automation

- **Repetitive hand-offs**: work that moves from one person or system to another on a schedule or on a trigger
- **Copying data between systems**: extracting from one source and entering into another on a predictable pattern
- **Routine status updates**: notifying the right people when something moves from one state to another
- **High-volume, rule-based decisions**: approvals that always follow the same criteria without edge cases

## Poor candidates for automation

- **Decisions that depend on judgement**: anything where the right answer requires understanding context that isn't in the data
- **Processes that change frequently**: automating something that will be redesigned next month spends more than it saves
- **Low-frequency tasks**: the overhead of building and maintaining an automation can exceed the time it saves

## When AI is involved

Keep a person in the loop for anything that affects people or money. Show where every answer came from. Measure mistakes in a small pilot before expanding the scope. AI automation that runs without oversight and without transparency creates liability, not efficiency.

The simplest automation that actually runs is better than the most sophisticated one that never gets finished.
